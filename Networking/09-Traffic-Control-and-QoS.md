# Chapter 9: Traffic Control and QoS

## 1. What this chapter is actually for

Chapter 2 introduced the queueing discipline, or qdisc, only briefly (section 5.2) — a checkpoint sitting between IP's routing decision and a driver's transmit function, mentioned mainly to explain why bandwidth shaping and packet reordering are possible at all. Chapters 3 through 8 each touched qdiscs in passing, as something deferred to this chapter. This is where that deferral gets paid off: what a qdisc actually is, how the several kinds Linux ships differ, how they compose into hierarchies, and how the entire mechanism connects to the concept of Quality of Service (QoS) — treating different traffic differently, deliberately, rather than serving everything on a strict first-come-first-served basis.

It's worth being explicit about why this sits at the end of the series' data-path chapters rather than earlier: traffic control is the one mechanism in this entire series that's fundamentally about *choosing what to sacrifice*, deliberately, under conditions of scarcity — every earlier chapter's mechanisms (routing, filtering, NAT, transport-layer reliability) were about correctly delivering traffic; this chapter is about correctly *delaying, reordering, or dropping* traffic, on purpose, when there isn't enough capacity to serve everything with equal priority at the same time. That's a genuinely different kind of problem, and it's the reason a chapter dedicated to it benefits from the full data-path model chapters 1 through 8 already built — deciding what to sacrifice only makes sense once the full picture of what's actually competing for that scarce capacity is already in view.

It's also worth naming a tension this chapter sits inside, since it shapes how its material should be read: traffic control is one of the more genuinely optional pieces of this entire series, in the sense that a very large fraction of Linux systems run indefinitely on nothing more than the unconfigured `pfifo_fast` or `fq_codel` default (section 2.1, 3.3) and never need anything more elaborate. The material in this chapter — HTB hierarchies, DSCP marking, ingress policing — earns its place specifically on links that are genuinely contested: a home internet connection shared between a video call and a large upload, a server's uplink serving both latency-sensitive and bulk traffic simultaneously, an ISP or enterprise network managing many customers' or departments' traffic on shared infrastructure. Recognizing whether a given situation is one of these genuinely-contested cases, rather than reaching for HTB configuration on an interface that was never actually short of capacity in the first place, is itself part of applying this chapter's material correctly.

## 2. Why a qdisc exists at all: the problem it's solving

Chapter 2's section 5.1 described the ordinary transmit path: IP hands a fully-formed packet toward an outbound `net_device`, which eventually reaches the driver's transmit function and, from there, a physical NIC's transmit ring. Left entirely to itself, with no scheduling policy imposed, this path would serve packets in whatever order they happened to arrive at the interface — a genuinely reasonable default when there's no meaningful contention (one connection, comfortably below the link's capacity), but a poor one the moment multiple flows compete for a link that can't serve all of them at their combined desired rate simultaneously.

### 2.1 The default: `pfifo_fast`

Every interface has a qdisc attached, even without any explicit configuration — chapter 2 mentioned this default is `pfifo_fast`, and it's worth understanding what it actually does, since it's the behavior every interface exhibits unless something else has been deliberately configured. `pfifo_fast` maintains three internal bands, and classifies packets into one of them based on their IP header's Type of Service (ToS) field (later standardized as the Differentiated Services Code Point, or DSCP, covered in section 4) — packets in a higher-priority band are always sent before any packet in a lower-priority band, but within a single band, ordering is strictly first-in-first-out, with no further distinction between flows sharing that band. This is a genuinely minimal form of traffic control — it does provide *some* differentiation (three priority levels, derived automatically from a header field applications can set), but it offers no actual rate limiting, no fairness guarantee between competing flows within the same band, and no configurability beyond what's baked into its fixed, three-band design.

### 2.2 What "differentiation" needs beyond `pfifo_fast`'s minimalism

The gap `pfifo_fast` leaves open is exactly what the rest of this chapter's mechanisms fill: rate limiting (capping how fast an interface, or a specific class of traffic on it, may transmit, even when the underlying hardware could go faster — section 3), fairness (ensuring one greedy flow doesn't starve others sharing the same link — section 3.3), and hierarchical policy (different bandwidth guarantees or limits for different categories of traffic, composed together rather than existing as a single flat priority scheme — section 5). Every qdisc beyond `pfifo_fast` that this chapter covers exists to address one or more of these three gaps specifically.

It's worth being precise about a distinction that recurs throughout the rest of this chapter: rate limiting and fairness solve genuinely different problems, and conflating them is a common source of misapplied configuration. Rate limiting answers "how much total bandwidth should this traffic be allowed to use," a question that makes sense even for a single flow with no competition at all — a video-streaming service deliberately capping its own upload rate to avoid overwhelming a residential connection's uplink, say, regardless of whether anything else happens to be competing for that same link at that moment. Fairness answers a different question entirely: "when multiple flows *are* competing for the same finite capacity, how should that capacity be divided between them" — a question that's meaningless in the single-flow case, since there's nothing to divide capacity between. A configuration that only rate-limits without addressing fairness can still let one flow within that rate-limited pool starve another; a configuration that only provides fairness without any rate limit at all can still let the aggregate of all flows combined saturate a link far beyond what's actually desired. The two concerns are complementary, not substitutes for each other, which is precisely why `fq_codel` (section 3.3) bundling fairness together with (indirectly, via CoDel) congestion signaling, while `tbf` (section 3.1) handles rate limiting alone, reflects two qdiscs solving two different halves of what a complete traffic-management policy actually needs.

## 3. Classless qdiscs: shaping without categorizing traffic first

Some qdiscs operate without needing to first sort packets into distinct classes — they apply a single, uniform policy to whatever traffic passes through them. These are simpler to reason about and are worth covering before the classful (hierarchical) qdiscs in section 5.

### 3.1 `tbf`: the token bucket filter

The **token bucket filter (`tbf`)** is the most direct implementation of rate limiting: a conceptual "bucket" accumulates tokens at a configured rate (the desired long-term bandwidth limit), and a packet may only be transmitted if enough tokens are available in the bucket to "pay" for its size, with those tokens then consumed — a packet arriving when insufficient tokens are available is held in a queue (up to a configured limit, beyond which packets are dropped) until enough tokens accumulate.

```
$ tc qdisc add dev eth0 root tbf rate 10mbit burst 32kbit latency 50ms
```

This configuration caps `eth0`'s outbound rate at 10 Mbit/s, with a `burst` parameter — the bucket's actual capacity — allowing short bursts above that sustained rate (up to the bucket's size, before the rate limit reasserts itself), and a `latency` parameter bounding how long a packet may wait in the queue before being dropped rather than transmitted late. The `burst` parameter is worth understanding precisely, because setting it too small defeats much of `tbf`'s practical usefulness: a bucket sized too small forces the rate limit to apply with no tolerance at all for the natural burstiness of real traffic (a web page load genuinely does arrive as a burst of many packets nearly simultaneously, not as a perfectly smooth, evenly-paced stream), producing more queueing delay and jitter than a correctly-sized bucket would, for traffic that would otherwise have been well within the intended long-term rate limit if merely permitted to arrive in its natural, bursty pattern.

It's worth being explicit about what `tbf` does and doesn't provide, since its simplicity is both its strength and its limitation: it enforces a single rate limit uniformly across all traffic passing through it, with no notion of different classes or priorities within that limit at all — a large file transfer and an interactive SSH session sharing the same `tbf`-limited interface are treated identically, competing for the same token bucket with no fairness mechanism distinguishing them (unlike `fq_codel`'s per-flow fairness, section 3.3). This makes `tbf` the right tool specifically when the goal is a single, uniform rate cap on an entire interface or a specific matched subset of traffic, and the wrong tool when different traffic categories sharing that link need to be treated differently from each other — that latter goal is exactly what section 5's classful qdiscs exist to address.

### 3.2 `netem`: simulating network conditions, not shaping them

**`netem`** (network emulation) is worth including here because it's frequently used in testing and development contexts, even though its purpose is different in kind from the other qdiscs in this chapter — rather than shaping real traffic to meet a policy goal, it deliberately introduces artificial delay, loss, duplication, or reordering, to let an application or protocol's behavior under adverse network conditions be tested on an otherwise pristine local or lab network:

```
$ tc qdisc add dev eth0 root netem delay 100ms 20ms loss 0.5%
```

This adds 100ms of latency (with 20ms of random variation, simulating jitter) and randomly drops 0.5% of packets — directly useful for confirming, for instance, that chapter 5's TCP retransmission and congestion-control machinery, or an application's own timeout handling, behaves sensibly under conditions considerably worse than a local test network would otherwise exhibit, without needing an actual degraded network link to test against. It's worth flagging clearly that `netem` is not something to leave configured on a production interface — its entire purpose is deliberately degrading a link for testing, and using it as a stand-in for genuine rate limiting or QoS policy would be applying the wrong tool for that goal, when `tbf` (section 3.1) or the classful qdiscs (section 5) exist specifically for actual traffic-shaping purposes.

### 3.3 `fq_codel`: fairness and bufferbloat mitigation together

**`fq_codel`** (Fair Queuing with Controlled Delay) deserves particular attention because it's the modern default qdisc on many Linux distributions for physical interfaces (visible, for instance, in chapter 3's earlier example output showing `qdisc fq_codel` on an ordinary interface), addressing both of the gaps section 2.2 identified — fairness and (implicitly) rate-adaptive queue management — in a single mechanism, without requiring any manual configuration to get meaningful benefit.

The "fair queuing" half separates traffic into many internal sub-queues, hashed by flow (source/destination address and port — conceptually similar to the flow-hashing chapter 2's RSS and chapter 3's ECMP sections both used, for a related underlying reason: keeping one flow's packets together while distributing load, here across queues rather than across CPU cores or paths), and serves those sub-queues in round-robin fashion — meaning one greedy, high-volume flow (a large file download, say) cannot monopolize the link at the expense of a smaller, latency-sensitive flow (a DNS query, an interactive SSH session) sharing the same interface, since each flow's sub-queue gets its fair turn regardless of how much data any other flow has queued.

The "controlled delay" half — CoDel, the algorithm `fq_codel` builds on — addresses a distinct problem worth explaining in its own right: **bufferbloat**, the phenomenon where an interface's queue grows large enough, under sustained load, to introduce substantial latency, even though no packets are actually being lost (a large queue can absorb a great deal of excess traffic without ever overflowing, simply by making every packet wait longer in line). CoDel monitors how long packets are actually sitting in the queue and begins dropping packets deliberately once that sojourn time exceeds a target threshold for a sustained period — not because the queue is full, but specifically to signal congestion back to TCP senders (chapter 5, section 4's congestion-control machinery reacting to exactly this kind of loss) before the queue grows large enough to introduce the latency bufferbloat is defined by. This is a genuinely elegant response to a problem that a naive, simply-larger-buffer approach makes worse rather than better — more buffer space alone doesn't prevent bufferbloat, since it's precisely large buffers filling gradually that produce it; what's needed is a mechanism, like CoDel, that recognizes and responds to a queue's *growing occupancy over time*, not merely to it eventually becoming completely full.

### 3.4 SFQ: a simpler precursor to `fq_codel`'s fairness half

It's worth mentioning **SFQ (Stochastic Fairness Queuing)** briefly, since it's an older, simpler qdisc addressing only the fairness half of what `fq_codel` (section 3.3) combines with delay-based congestion signaling. SFQ hashes flows into a fixed number of internal queues (the "stochastic" in its name reflecting that this hashing can, rarely, place two genuinely different flows into the same internal queue by collision, a limitation `fq_codel`'s design also inherits but manages somewhat more gracefully) and serves those queues in round-robin fashion, exactly as `fq_codel`'s fair-queuing half does — but without CoDel's delay-based dropping, meaning SFQ alone provides fairness between flows without doing anything to address bufferbloat specifically. This is worth knowing mainly as historical and comparative context: `fq_codel`'s adoption as the modern default reflects a judgment that fairness alone, without bufferbloat mitigation, leaves a real problem unaddressed, and that combining both concerns into one qdisc is preferable to choosing between two separate, narrower ones.

## 4. DSCP and traffic marking: how a packet expresses its own priority



Section 2.1 mentioned that `pfifo_fast` classifies packets using the IP header's ToS/DSCP field. It's worth understanding this field's role more fully, because it's the mechanism by which a packet can carry an indication of its own intended priority, independent of whatever qdisc eventually acts on that indication.

### 4.1 What DSCP actually is

The **Differentiated Services Code Point (DSCP)** occupies six bits of the IP header's ToS byte (chapter 1's original IP header discussion mentioned this byte only in passing), and a small number of standardized DSCP values have widely recognized conventional meanings — `EF` (Expedited Forwarding), typically used for latency-sensitive traffic like voice, signaling "this traffic wants minimal queueing delay, even at the expense of throughput"; several `AF` (Assured Forwarding) classes, offering different combinations of guaranteed throughput and drop precedence for traffic that can tolerate more latency but wants bandwidth assurance; and a default, unmarked value for ordinary best-effort traffic with no particular priority request at all.

### 4.2 Marking traffic: Netfilter's role, tying back to chapter 4

Setting a packet's DSCP value is, mechanically, exactly the kind of header-mangling operation chapter 4's `mangle` table (section 3) was described as handling — a rule can set DSCP based on essentially any criteria Netfilter can match (source, destination, port, application-layer inspection results), independent of and prior to whatever qdisc later acts on that marking:

```
$ iptables -t mangle -A OUTPUT -p udp --dport 5060 -j DSCP --set-dscp-class EF
```

This marks outbound SIP signaling traffic (a common real-world case for `EF` marking) as latency-sensitive, and a downstream qdisc — either on this machine, or, more usefully, on routers further along the path that also respect DSCP markings — can then act on that marking to prioritize the traffic's actual forwarding treatment. It's worth being clear about a genuine limitation this implies: DSCP marking is only as useful as the cooperation of every device along the path that's expected to honor it — a marking set correctly on an originating machine has no effect at all on a router further along the path that either ignores DSCP entirely or, common on much public internet infrastructure, deliberately strips or rewrites it (a policy some networks adopt specifically to prevent traffic from claiming a priority its originator wasn't actually entitled to). This is precisely why DSCP-based QoS is considerably more reliable and commonly deployed *within* a single administrative domain (an enterprise's own internal network, where every hop's configuration is under one operator's control) than across the broader, uncooperative public internet, where markings frequently don't survive the full path intact.

### 4.3 The distinction between marking and enforcement, restated plainly

It's worth closing this section with a distinction that's easy to blur given how closely sections 4.1 and 4.2 sit together: marking a packet's DSCP value and actually enforcing differentiated treatment based on that value are two entirely separate steps, performed by entirely separate mechanisms. Netfilter's `mangle` table (section 4.2) only ever *sets* the marking — it has no role whatsoever in actually queueing, delaying, or prioritizing anything. The qdisc — `pfifo_fast`'s built-in DSCP-aware banding (section 2.1), or an HTB filter explicitly matching on DSCP (section 5.2) — is what actually *acts* on a marking already present. A configuration that sets DSCP correctly via `iptables` but attaches no qdisc capable of honoring that marking accomplishes nothing observable at all, exactly the kind of two-step, easy-to-half-configure mechanism this series has flagged repeatedly in other contexts (chapter 4's NAT/filtering split, chapter 3's routing-rule/route split) — marking without enforcement, or enforcement without correct marking, each independently produce no useful effect, and both halves need to be correctly in place simultaneously for DSCP-based prioritization to actually change anything about how traffic is handled.

## 5. Classful qdiscs: composing hierarchical policy



Section 3's qdiscs each apply one uniform policy to all traffic passing through them. **Classful qdiscs** generalize this into a hierarchy: traffic is first sorted into classes (based on filters, which can match on essentially anything Netfilter or the DSCP marking from section 4 could — source/destination, port, or an explicit fwmark set earlier in the pipeline, tying directly back to chapter 3's section 5.3 discussion of `fwmark`-based selection), and each class can have its own distinct qdisc and its own bandwidth allocation, with the overall hierarchy determining how contested bandwidth gets shared between classes when the link is under pressure.

### 5.1 HTB: the hierarchical token bucket

**HTB (Hierarchical Token Bucket)** is the most commonly used classful qdisc for exactly this kind of hierarchical bandwidth allocation. Each class in an HTB hierarchy is configured with a *rate* (its guaranteed minimum bandwidth) and a *ceiling* (the maximum it may use, including bandwidth borrowed from sibling classes that aren't currently using their own full guaranteed rate) — a structure that lets an administrator express policy like "the VoIP class always gets at least 1 Mbit/s, and may burst up to 2 Mbit/s if bandwidth is available, while the bulk-download class gets whatever's left over, up to the link's full capacity, when the VoIP class isn't using its guarantee":

```
$ tc qdisc add dev eth0 root handle 1: htb default 30
$ tc class add dev eth0 parent 1: classid 1:1 htb rate 10mbit
$ tc class add dev eth0 parent 1:1 classid 1:10 htb rate 2mbit ceil 3mbit
$ tc class add dev eth0 parent 1:1 classid 1:30 htb rate 1mbit ceil 10mbit
$ tc filter add dev eth0 parent 1: protocol ip u32 match ip dport 5060 0xffff flowid 1:10
```

This creates a root class (`1:1`) representing the interface's total 10 Mbit/s capacity, with two child classes beneath it — `1:10` (matched to SIP traffic via the filter, guaranteed 2 Mbit/s, capable of bursting to 3 Mbit/s) and `1:30` (the default class for everything else, guaranteed only 1 Mbit/s but able to borrow up to the full 10 Mbit/s when the SIP class isn't using its own guarantee). This borrowing behavior — a class using more than its guaranteed rate specifically because a sibling isn't currently using its own share — is HTB's defining feature, and it's what distinguishes genuinely hierarchical bandwidth *sharing* from simple, independent per-class rate limits that would waste capacity whenever one class's traffic happened to be idle.

### 5.2 Why filters, not just classes, determine actual behavior

It's worth being explicit that an HTB hierarchy's classes alone don't determine which traffic goes where — the **filters** attached to the hierarchy (the `tc filter add` line above, using the `u32` classifier to match on the UDP destination port) are what actually sort incoming packets into the correct class, and a hierarchy with correctly-configured classes but a missing or incorrect filter will simply send all unmatched traffic to whatever class is configured as the `default` (here, `1:30`), regardless of how carefully the other classes' rates and ceilings were tuned. This mirrors a theme that has recurred throughout this series — chapter 4's warning that a syntactically correct Netfilter rule in the wrong chain does nothing, and chapter 3's warning that a routing rule's ordering can silently misdirect traffic — applied here to traffic control specifically: the classification step (which traffic goes to which class) is just as load-bearing, and just as easy to get subtly wrong, as the rate/ceiling configuration of the classes themselves.

### 5.3 A worked example: reasoning through borrowing behavior

It's worth tracing through HTB's borrowing behavior (section 5.1) with concrete numbers, since the interaction between guaranteed rate and ceiling is easier to internalize with an example than from the abstract description alone. Using section 5.1's configuration — a 10 Mbit/s link, with the SIP class (`1:10`) guaranteed 2 Mbit/s and capped at 3 Mbit/s, and the default class (`1:30`) guaranteed 1 Mbit/s but capable of borrowing up to the full 10 Mbit/s:

- If SIP traffic is idle and the default class has 8 Mbit/s of traffic to send, it can use the entire remaining 9 Mbit/s of link capacity (the full 10 Mbit/s, since nothing else is competing for it, bounded only by its own 10 Mbit/s ceiling) — well beyond its own 1 Mbit/s guarantee, precisely because that guarantee is a *minimum*, not a cap, when siblings aren't using their own share.
- The moment SIP traffic begins flowing, needing up to its own 2 Mbit/s guarantee, HTB reclaims that bandwidth from whatever the default class was borrowing, reducing the default class's actual share correspondingly — the default class doesn't get to keep bandwidth it was only ever borrowing once the class it belonged to actually needs it back.
- If SIP traffic needs more than its 2 Mbit/s guarantee — up to its own 3 Mbit/s ceiling — it can, in turn, borrow from the default class's own unused capacity, exactly the same borrowing relationship in reverse, provided the default class isn't itself using its own full guarantee at that moment.

This example is worth holding in mind because it clarifies a point that's easy to get backward when first encountering HTB: *rate* is a floor, guaranteed regardless of what siblings are doing, while *ceiling* is merely a cap on how much can be borrowed *beyond* that floor when capacity happens to be available — neither value alone determines a class's actual, moment-to-moment bandwidth; that's always a function of both the class's own configuration and its siblings' current, competing demand.

### 5.4 CBQ and PRIO: other classful qdiscs, briefly

HTB is not the only classful qdisc Linux provides, though it's the most commonly reached for today for hierarchical bandwidth allocation specifically. **CBQ (Class Based Queuing)**, an older mechanism predating HTB, aimed at similar goals but through a considerably more complex, link-utilization-estimation-based algorithm that proved difficult to tune correctly in practice — HTB's more direct, explicit rate/ceiling model was developed specifically as a more predictable, easier-to-reason-about alternative, and has largely superseded CBQ in contemporary use as a result. **PRIO**, by contrast, is a considerably simpler classful qdisc, offering strict priority between a small, fixed number of classes (packets in a higher-priority class always transmitted before any packet in a lower one, with no borrowing or rate-sharing concept at all) — closer in spirit to `pfifo_fast`'s three-band model (section 2.1) than to HTB's bandwidth-sharing hierarchy, and appropriate specifically when the goal is strict ordering between traffic categories rather than proportional bandwidth sharing between them.

## 6. Ingress traffic control: policing rather than shaping



Everything described in sections 2 through 5 governs *outbound* (egress) traffic, where the local machine has direct, deterministic control over exactly when a packet actually leaves — it can hold a packet in a qdisc's queue for as long as needed before transmitting it. Incoming (ingress) traffic is fundamentally different in this respect: a packet has already arrived, having already consumed whatever bandwidth it used on the way in, by the time any local traffic-control mechanism gets a chance to act on it — there's no way to "queue" a packet's arrival the way outbound traffic can be queued before departure, since the sending side, not the receiving side, controls when and how fast packets are actually transmitted onto the wire.

Because of this asymmetry, ingress traffic control on Linux is generally implemented as **policing** rather than shaping: rather than smoothly queueing traffic to conform to a rate, a policer simply drops packets that exceed a configured rate immediately upon arrival, providing a comparatively blunt instrument relative to egress shaping's more graceful queueing-based approach. It's worth being clear about why this bluntness is an inherent property of ingress control rather than a mere implementation limitation: shaping's smoothness comes specifically from the ability to hold a packet briefly rather than transmit or drop it immediately, and that ability simply doesn't exist for a packet that has already fully arrived — there's no way to make a sender's already-completed transmission arrive more slowly after the fact, only to decide, upon arrival, whether to keep or discard what already came in. A more commonly used technique for effectively influencing an incoming rate, given this limitation, involves an **Intermediate Functional Block (IFB)** device — a virtual interface that ingress traffic can be redirected through, allowing the full range of egress qdiscs (including HTB's hierarchical shaping, section 5.1) to be applied to what is, from the IFB's own perspective, now outbound traffic, even though it originated as this machine's inbound traffic — a genuine, if somewhat indirect, workaround for egress-shaping's fundamental unavailability on true ingress traffic.

## 7. What's deliberately being deferred

- The specific `u32` classifier syntax used in section 5.1's filter example, and the considerably richer set of classifier options `tc` supports (including `ebpf`-based classifiers, connecting back to the eBPF preview in chapters 1 and 4), are not covered in further depth here — this chapter's focus is the qdisc hierarchy's behavior, not the full classifier configuration language.
- Wireless-specific QoS mechanisms (802.11e/WMM, which apply similar prioritization concepts at the Wi-Fi link layer specifically) operate under constraints particular to shared-medium wireless transmission that this chapter, focused on Ethernet-oriented wired traffic control, doesn't address.
- Router-to-router QoS protocols (DiffServ deployment across a multi-hop network, as opposed to the single-interface qdisc configuration this chapter covers) and the broader question of end-to-end QoS guarantees across an administrative boundary are a considerably larger topic than this chapter's single-machine, single-interface scope.

## 8. Glossary of terms introduced in this chapter

- **qdisc (queueing discipline)** — the configurable queueing/scheduling layer governing which packet an interface transmits next; every interface has one, defaulting to `pfifo_fast` (sections 2.1, chapter 2 section 5.2).
- **`tbf` (token bucket filter)** — a classless qdisc implementing straightforward rate limiting via an accumulating token bucket (section 3.1).
- **`netem`** — a qdisc for deliberately introducing artificial delay, loss, or reordering, for testing rather than production traffic shaping (section 3.2).
- **`fq_codel`** — the modern default qdisc combining per-flow fair queuing with CoDel's delay-based, bufferbloat-mitigating drop behavior (section 3.3).
- **SFQ (Stochastic Fairness Queuing)** — an older, simpler qdisc providing per-flow fairness alone, without `fq_codel`'s additional bufferbloat mitigation (section 3.4).
- **bufferbloat** — excessive queueing latency arising from large buffers absorbing sustained excess traffic without ever overflowing enough to signal congestion through ordinary loss (section 3.3).
- **DSCP (Differentiated Services Code Point)** — a field in the IP header's ToS byte expressing a packet's requested priority treatment, honored only to the extent every hop along a path cooperates with it (section 4).
- **HTB (Hierarchical Token Bucket)** — the standard classful qdisc for hierarchical bandwidth allocation, supporting guaranteed rates and borrowable ceilings between sibling classes (section 5.1).
- **CBQ / PRIO** — an older, more complex classful qdisc largely superseded by HTB, and a simpler strict-priority classful qdisc without bandwidth-sharing between classes, respectively (section 5.4).
- **policing** — the ingress-side analog to egress shaping, dropping excess traffic immediately on arrival rather than queueing it, due to the receiving side's inability to control when traffic was actually sent (section 6).
- **IFB (Intermediate Functional Block)** — a virtual device allowing ingress traffic to be treated as egress traffic for the purposes of applying ordinary shaping qdiscs to it (section 6).

## 9. A closing note connecting this back to the rest of the series

Chapter 2 introduced the qdisc as a checkpoint sitting between a routing decision and a driver's transmit function, without elaborating on what happens at that checkpoint. This chapter has, hopefully, filled that gap in completely: `pfifo_fast`'s minimal three-band priority scheme is the unconfigured default every interface already has; `tbf`, `netem`, and `fq_codel` each address a specific classless traffic-shaping need — rate limiting, deliberate degradation for testing, and fairness-plus-bufferbloat-mitigation respectively; DSCP marking, set via exactly the Netfilter `mangle` table mechanism chapter 4 introduced, is how a packet expresses its own requested priority to whatever qdisc or downstream router chooses to honor it; and HTB generalizes all of this into a genuine hierarchy, letting different traffic classes share a link's capacity according to policy rather than raw arrival order, with the same classification concerns — get the filter wrong, and the carefully-tuned classes never see the traffic they were meant for — that chapters 3 and 4 already warned about in their own respective contexts.

This chapter also completes something worth naming explicitly as this series' data-path arc comes to a close: chapter 2 traced a packet's journey through DMA rings, NAPI polling, and `sk_buff` allocation; chapter 3 traced how that packet's destination gets decided; chapter 4 traced how policy gets applied to it via Netfilter; chapter 5 traced what the transport layer does with it once routing and filtering are settled; chapters 6 through 8 traced how a destination address gets found in the first place, how an isolated namespace's traffic finds its way out, and how a tunnel disguises a packet for an untrusted path — and this chapter has traced the final piece: given everything else already decided, in what order, and at what rate, a packet actually leaves. Chapter 10 turns to hardening — the sysctl-level knobs that adjust the behavior of nearly every mechanism this series has covered, examined specifically for what each one actually defends against and what tradeoff accepting it involves, rather than as an undifferentiated checklist of settings to enable.
