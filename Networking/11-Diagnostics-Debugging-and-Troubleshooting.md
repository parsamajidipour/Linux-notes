# Chapter 11: Diagnostics, Debugging, and Troubleshooting

## 1. What this chapter is actually for

Every chapter in this series has ended with its own closing diagnostic sequence — chapter 2's five-step check for unexplained packet loss, chapter 3's four-step routing checklist, chapter 6's four-step DNS resolution sequence, chapter 7's container-networking checklist, chapter 8's MTU diagnostic, and so on. This final chapter's job is not to repeat any of those in detail, but to assemble them into one coherent method: given an arbitrary, unclassified "the network isn't working" report, where does a systematic investigation actually start, and how does it move from a vague symptom to one of this series' precise, chapter-specific diagnostic sequences.

This is deliberately a chapter about method, not about new mechanism. Everything it draws on has already been built in chapters 1 through 10; what's been missing is the connective structure — the decision tree that takes a symptom in, and routes the investigation toward whichever chapter's specific tools the symptom actually calls for, rather than either guessing at a cause or working through every chapter's checklist regardless of relevance.

It's worth stating plainly what kind of value this chapter is meant to add, since it introduces no new mechanism of its own: the previous ten chapters, read individually, each equip a reader to diagnose problems within that chapter's own specific domain — but a person facing a genuinely unclassified symptom doesn't know, in advance, which domain the problem actually belongs to. That's precisely the gap this chapter closes. The method built here is what turns ten separate, excellent domain-specific toolkits into a single, usable investigative practice, applicable the moment a symptom arrives with no prior indication of where its actual root cause lies.

## 2. The layered elimination principle

The single organizing idea underlying everything in this chapter is one this series has used implicitly from chapter 1 onward: chapter 1's four-layer model (link, network, transport, application) is not just a way of organizing what the kernel does — it's a diagnostic order. A problem at a lower layer produces symptoms that can look, superficially, like problems at every layer above it (a downed interface can look like "the application won't connect," exactly as a firewall rule or a DNS failure can), but a problem at a higher layer cannot masquerade as a lower-layer failure, because the lower layers' correct operation is a precondition for the higher layers ever running at all. This asymmetry is what makes bottom-up elimination the efficient order to investigate in: confirming the link layer works rules out an entire category of possible explanations at once, in a way that confirming the application layer works could never do for the layers underneath it.

### 2.1 The four questions, in order

Given an unclassified connectivity complaint, the four questions worth asking, strictly in this order, are:

1. **Is the link up and correctly addressed?** (chapters 1 and 3) — does the relevant interface report as administratively up, does it hold the address it's expected to, and is the physical or virtual medium underneath it actually functional?
2. **Is the packet being routed and permitted?** (chapters 3 and 4) — does a routing lookup for the actual destination in question produce the expected outbound interface and next hop, and does Netfilter, at whichever hooks are relevant, actually permit this specific traffic through?
3. **Is the transport layer establishing and maintaining a connection correctly?** (chapter 5) — is a TCP handshake completing, is data flowing at a reasonable rate given the path's actual characteristics, and is the connection's state consistent with what's expected?
4. **Is the application-level protocol behaving correctly given a working connection?** (largely outside this series' scope, but worth naming as the question that remains once questions 1 through 3 are all answered affirmatively) — is the actual data being exchanged what both ends expect, independent of anything this series' networking-layer material can further illuminate?

A report of "I can't reach the server" almost always turns out, on investigation, to be a failure at question 1 or 2 — link/routing/filtering problems are simply more common in practice than genuine transport-layer or application-layer failures, precisely because they're also the layers most frequently touched by configuration changes (a new firewall rule, a route added or removed, an interface reconfigured). This is why the sequence in section 2.1 is ordered the way it is: it moves through the *statistically* most likely explanations first, which happens to coincide with the layers a lower-layer failure could most broadly disguise itself as.

### 2.2 Why the reverse order fails as a diagnostic strategy

It's worth being explicit about why starting from the top (application symptoms) and working downward is the less efficient order, even though it's the order most naturally suggested by how a problem is initially reported — someone experiences "the app can't connect," which is an application-layer observation, and the natural instinct is to start investigating at that same layer. The issue is that an application-layer symptom carries very little information about *which* layer actually failed — a connection timeout looks identical whether caused by a downed interface, a missing route, a firewall rule, a stalled TCP handshake, or a genuinely unresponsive remote application. Starting the investigation at the application layer means investigating a layer that, by itself, cannot distinguish between any of these very different underlying causes, whereas starting at the link layer (section 3) and working upward means each confirmed-healthy layer genuinely eliminates a specific, large category of possible explanations before the investigation ever needs to consider the layers above it.

## 3. Question 1 in practice: link and addressing



Chapter 1's section 5 and chapter 3's sections 2 through 2.3 already built the specific commands for this question; this section's job is narrower — establishing this as genuinely the first thing to check, before anything more elaborate, and being precise about what a clean result here actually rules out.

### 3.1 The minimal check, and what a clean result means

`ip link show <interface>` confirming `UP` and `LOWER_UP` flags, plus `ip addr show <interface>` confirming the expected address is present, rules out an entire category of explanations: a downed interface, a missing or wrong address (chapter 3, section 2.3's warning about addresses persisting on a downed interface applies directly — checking only `ip addr` without also checking `ip link`'s up/down state can produce a false sense of security), or, for a physical interface, a disconnected or faulty physical link (`LOWER_UP`'s absence specifically indicates no physical or virtual carrier signal detected, distinct from the interface merely being administratively down).

### 3.2 Why this check is cheap and should never be skipped

It's worth being explicit about why this question belongs first even when a more "interesting" hypothesis (a routing misconfiguration, a firewall rule) seems more likely given the specific symptom being reported: this check costs seconds to perform and rules out, with near-total confidence, an entire category of possible explanations, for a cost low enough that skipping it in favor of a more elaborate hypothesis is rarely justified, even when that more elaborate hypothesis eventually turns out to be correct. A downed interface producing symptoms that superficially resemble a routing or application-layer problem is common enough, and this check cheap enough, that it belongs at the start of every investigation regardless of how confident the investigator feels about a different, more specific hypothesis.

## 4. Question 2 in practice: routing and filtering

This is the question with the largest toolkit built across this series, and it's worth being systematic about which specific check addresses which specific sub-question, since "check routing and filtering" is still too broad a single step to execute directly.

### 4.1 Routing: ask the kernel, don't reason about the table by eye

Chapter 3, section 3.4 built exactly the right tool for this: `ip route get <destination>` (with an explicit `from <source>` if policy routing, chapter 3 section 5, might be involved) reports the actual routing decision the kernel would make, without requiring manual longest-prefix-match reasoning against a potentially large or policy-routed table. A result that doesn't match expectations — wrong interface, wrong next hop, or an unexpected table being consulted — points directly at chapter 3's material: a missing or incorrect route, a rule-ordering problem (chapter 3, section 5.4), or a stale route referencing a torn-down interface (chapter 3, section 6.2's ifindex-churn discussion).

### 4.2 Neighbor resolution: a distinct, often-overlooked sub-question

Chapter 3, section 4's neighbor subsystem is worth checking as its own distinct step, separate from the routing lookup itself, precisely because a correct routing decision doesn't guarantee the resolved next hop is actually reachable at the link layer. `ip neigh show` reporting a `FAILED` or persistently `INCOMPLETE` entry for the relevant next hop, on an Ethernet-type interface, points at a genuine link-layer resolution problem (chapter 3, section 4.1's NUD state machine) distinct from anything the routing table itself got wrong — a distinction worth holding onto specifically because a correct route pointing at an unreachable next hop produces symptoms (packets simply vanish, with no explicit error) that can otherwise be mistaken for a routing misconfiguration when the routing decision itself was actually correct all along.

### 4.3 Filtering: chain, table, and hit counters, in that order

Chapter 4 built a specific sequence worth reapplying here directly: confirm which chain (chapter 4, section 2) the traffic in question actually traverses given its direction (locally-destined, forwarded, or locally-originated), confirm which table (chapter 4, section 3) governs the relevant policy, and then check rule-hit counters (chapter 4, section 10.1) to confirm a specific rule is actually being reached and matched as expected, rather than assuming from the rule's presence in the ruleset that it's necessarily being evaluated at all. A rule that's syntactically present but sitting in the wrong chain, or shadowed by an earlier, broader rule in the same chain, produces zero hits despite looking correct on inspection — exactly the failure mode chapter 4's entire structure was built to make legible.

### 4.4 Bridged and namespaced traffic: an easy-to-miss variant

Chapter 4, section 8, and chapter 7's sections 4.3 and 5.2, both flagged a specific variant of this same question worth checking explicitly whenever containers or bridges are involved: is the traffic in question genuinely reaching the Netfilter hooks at all, given that bridged traffic bypasses them entirely without `br_netfilter` enabled, and a container's own namespace-scoped Netfilter state is entirely separate from the host's. A firewall rule that looks correct, in the right chain and table, still does nothing if the traffic it's meant to govern never reaches that hook in the first place — this specific check (`sysctl net.bridge.bridge-nf-call-iptables`, and confirming which namespace a given rule was actually applied in) belongs in this question's checklist specifically for container and bridge-involved topologies.

### 4.5 A note on searching for the change, not just the symptom

It's worth adding a habit to this question's checklist that isn't tied to any single chapter's mechanism but recurs across all of them: a networking problem that appears suddenly, on a system that was previously working, is far more often explained by *something that changed* than by a previously-latent bug finally manifesting. Before working through sections 4.1 through 4.4's checks from scratch, it's worth asking directly what changed recently — a new firewall rule deployed, a route added or removed, a new container or namespace created, a kernel or driver update, a network topology change on the physical infrastructure. This doesn't replace the systematic checks in sections 4.1 through 4.4; it complements them, often narrowing which of those checks is actually worth performing first. A firewall rule deployed an hour before the symptom began is a considerably more efficient starting hypothesis than beginning section 4's investigation with no prior information at all, even though the systematic checks would eventually find the same root cause regardless of where the investigation started.

## 5. Question 3 in practice: the transport layer



Chapter 5 built the primary tool for this question directly: `ss -tin` (chapter 5, section 2.2), reporting a connection's actual state, congestion window, retransmission timeout, and cumulative byte counts, rather than inferring transport-layer health from application-level symptoms alone.

### 5.1 Distinguishing "never connects" from "connects but performs poorly"

These are genuinely different questions, addressed by different parts of this series. A connection that never reaches `ESTABLISHED` at all (chapter 1, section 8) points back toward question 2 — something in routing or filtering is preventing the handshake from completing, and the investigation should return to section 4 rather than proceeding further into transport-layer-specific territory. A connection that does establish, but performs poorly (low throughput, high latency, stalls under load), is where chapter 5's specific machinery — the congestion window, RTO, and buffer-size fields `ss -tin` reports — becomes the relevant diagnostic surface, and where chapter 5's closing point bears repeating: TCP's adaptive behavior, correctly reacting to genuinely poor underlying network conditions, is not itself a bug to be fixed, and distinguishing "TCP is behaving exactly as it should given a genuinely bad path" from "something is misconfigured" requires understanding chapter 5's actual mechanisms rather than assuming poor performance always indicates a fixable configuration problem.

### 5.2 When the tunnel or virtual-interface layer is involved

If the connection in question traverses a tunnel (chapter 8) or a virtual/namespaced interface (chapter 7), this question's investigation needs those chapters' own specific checks folded in: chapter 8's MTU diagnostic sequence (section 5.2) for a tunnel exhibiting the "small packets fine, large packets stall" signature specifically, or chapter 8's `wg show`/`ip xfrm state` checks (sections 3.3, 4.3) for confirming the tunnel itself is actually passing traffic, before attributing a performance problem to ordinary TCP behavior on what might, in fact, be a tunnel that has silently stopped functioning at all.

### 5.3 A quick reference: what a healthy `ss -tin` looks like versus what doesn't

It's worth being concrete about what "checking `ss -tin`" actually means in practice, since the raw field names alone don't convey what a healthy versus unhealthy result looks like. A connection with a low, stable RTT relative to the known physical distance between endpoints, a congestion window that has grown to a reasonable size for the connection's age and hasn't been repeatedly and recently cut (chapter 5, section 4.3's sawtooth pattern, visible as a `cwnd` value that's small relative to how long the connection has been open), and a `retrans` count of zero or near-zero, is behaving normally — whatever the application-layer symptom, the transport layer itself is healthy and the investigation should look elsewhere (typically back to question 2, or forward to question 4). A connection showing a `cwnd` that's collapsed to a small value recently, or a nonzero and climbing `retrans` count, or an RTT with high variance relative to its own mean, is showing genuine transport-layer distress — and, per section 5.1's point, the right next step is investigating *why* (a genuinely lossy or congested path, which points the investigation toward chapter 2's lower-level counters, as illustrated in section 8's worked example) rather than assuming the transport-layer symptom itself is the root cause requiring a transport-layer fix.

## 6. Question 4, briefly: recognizing where this series' scope ends



Once questions 1 through 3 are all answered affirmatively — link up and addressed correctly, routing and filtering both permitting the traffic as expected, and a TCP connection established and behaving reasonably given the path's actual characteristics — a remaining problem is, by elimination, an application-layer issue: the data being exchanged over an otherwise-healthy connection isn't what one or both ends expect. This is worth naming explicitly as the point where this series' material stops being directly applicable, not because application-layer debugging is unimportant, but because it's a genuinely different subject (protocol-specific behavior, application logic, data formatting) rather than a further layer of networking mechanism this series would be the right place to cover. Recognizing that a problem has reached this boundary — rather than continuing to look for a networking-layer explanation for what's actually an application-layer issue — is itself a useful outcome of working through the first three questions systematically: it directs further investigation toward the right domain, rather than leaving it stuck searching for a networking explanation that doesn't exist.

## 7. DNS as an orthogonal concern, not a numbered question

Chapter 6's material deserves a distinct mention here because DNS resolution doesn't fit cleanly into the four-question sequence above — it's not a layer in the chapter 1 sense, but a service consulted, usually, before any of questions 1 through 4 even become relevant, since a connection can't be attempted at all if the destination address was never successfully resolved. A "can't reach the server" report is worth checking, before question 1, for whether the destination was ever correctly resolved in the first place — chapter 6's `getent hosts` (section 2.2), checked against what `dig` reports, immediately reveals whether the actual problem is DNS-layer (a stale cache, an `/etc/hosts` override, a `search`-suffix mismatch) rather than anything in questions 1 through 4's territory at all. This is worth treating as an explicit zeroth check specifically because a DNS failure produces symptoms — "can't connect," "times out" — that are otherwise indistinguishable, from the outside, from a genuine question-1-through-3 networking failure, until the specific check (does the name even resolve to the expected address) is actually performed.

## 8. A single worked investigation, drawing on the whole series

It's worth tracing one complete, realistic investigation end to end, showing how the pieces from sections 2 through 7 compose in practice rather than being applied as isolated, disconnected checks.

**Symptom**: a container's application intermittently fails to reach an external API over HTTPS, with no clear pattern to when it fails.

1. **DNS check first** (section 7): `getent hosts api.example.com` from inside the container's namespace (chapter 7, section 5.3) returns a consistent address across repeated checks — DNS is not the intermittent factor here, ruling out chapter 6's territory as the primary cause.
2. **Question 1** (section 3): the container's `veth` interface (chapter 7, section 3) and the host's bridge (chapter 7, section 4) both report as up, with expected addresses — link and addressing check out cleanly.
3. **Question 2, routing** (section 4.1): `ip netns exec <container-ns> ip route get <resolved-address>` correctly shows the container's default route pointing at the bridge, and the host's own routing table correctly shows a route to the real external destination via the physical uplink — routing checks out at both the container and host layers.
4. **Question 2, filtering** (section 4.3): rule-hit counters on the host's `FORWARD` chain show the relevant NAT and filtering rules are being hit consistently, with no unexpected drop counts — filtering isn't rejecting this traffic.
5. **Question 3** (section 5): `ss -tin` inside the container, checked specifically *during* one of the intermittent failures (rather than only when the connection happens to be working), reveals the telling detail: the connection's `retrans` count is elevated, and its RTT samples show unusually high variance — pointing at genuine, intermittent packet loss on the actual path, rather than anything in questions 1 or 2's configuration-based territory at all.
6. Given step 5's finding, the investigation shifts from "what's misconfigured" to "what's causing intermittent loss on this specific path" — checking `/proc/net/softnet_stat` (chapter 2, section 3.2) on the host reveals periodic budget-exhaustion events correlating with the container's failure timestamps, indicating the host's own NAPI processing is intermittently falling behind under load, dropping packets at the driver level (chapter 2, sections 2 through 3) before they ever reach the container-bound path questions 1 through 4 were checking further up the stack.

This single trace touches chapters 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 in sequence, and it's worth noting what made the investigation efficient rather than a matter of luck: each step either confirmed a layer was healthy (ruling out an entire category of explanation cheaply) or, at step 5, produced a specific, actionable signal (`retrans` and RTT variance) that redirected the investigation toward the actual root cause, considerably below where the symptom itself was being observed. Nothing about this trace required guessing; each step's outcome directly determined the next one, exactly as sections 2 through 7's layered elimination principle intends.

It's worth also noting what this trace would have looked like had the investigation started in the wrong place — skipping straight to step 5's transport-layer check, for instance, purely because "intermittent" sounds like a transport-layer word. Nothing would have gone *wrong* with that shortcut in this particular case, since step 5 did turn out to hold the key signal — but the investigator would have arrived there without the confidence that steps 1 through 4 provide: the assurance that DNS, addressing, routing, and filtering are all genuinely healthy, rather than merely unexamined. In a case where the actual root cause turned out to lie in routing or filtering instead, skipping directly to a transport-layer check would have found nothing wrong there (correctly, since nothing was wrong there) while leaving the investigator without a clear next step, having skipped the layers that would have actually revealed the problem. The systematic order isn't merely a stylistic preference; it's what guarantees that a "nothing wrong here" result at any given step is trustworthy, rather than merely reflecting that the investigation hadn't yet looked in the right place.

## 9. What this chapter, and this series, has deliberately not covered

- Application-layer protocol debugging (HTTP semantics, TLS handshake failures beyond the transport-layer connection they ride on, API-specific error handling) is genuinely outside this series' networking-mechanism scope, as section 6 flagged directly.
- Performance profiling beyond the specific counters and tools this series has already introduced (chapter 2's `mpstat`/softirq checks, chapter 5's `ss -tin`) — a genuinely deep performance investigation can involve considerably more specialized tooling (perf, eBPF-based tracing) than this series has covered.
- Multi-host, cross-network diagnosis (tracing a problem that spans several administrative domains, where full visibility into every hop isn't available) inherits this chapter's method but faces practical limits this single-machine-focused series hasn't addressed.

It's worth being explicit about why these boundaries were drawn where they were, rather than simply listing them as omissions. Application-layer debugging is a genuinely different discipline, closer to software debugging in general than to the kernel-level networking mechanism this series has focused on throughout — the skills involved (reading application logs, understanding a specific protocol's semantics, reasoning about business logic) don't build on chapters 1 through 10 in the way each of those chapters builds on the ones before it. Advanced performance profiling, similarly, is a discipline in its own right, with its own considerable body of technique (flame graphs, sampling profilers, kernel tracing frameworks) that would need its own dedicated treatment to cover properly rather than being folded into a single closing chapter. And multi-host diagnosis, while it genuinely does extend this chapter's layered-elimination method, introduces a wholly new complication — the investigator's own visibility being incomplete, since not every hop along a multi-host path is necessarily under the investigator's own administrative control — that a single-machine-focused series was never going to be able to fully resolve. Each of these boundaries, in other words, marks not an oversight but a genuine edge of what a coherent, single series focused on one machine's kernel-level networking mechanism can reasonably cover.

## 10. A closing note for the series as a whole

This series began, in chapter 1, with a four-layer model and a promise: build a mental model precise enough that a specific configuration change becomes derivable rather than guessed, and a specific failure becomes diagnosable through direct observation rather than folklore. Ten chapters later, that model has been filled in completely, from a frame's electrical signal arriving at a NIC (chapter 2) through routing and filtering (chapters 3 and 4), the transport layer (chapter 5), name resolution (chapter 6), namespace isolation and virtual interfaces (chapter 7), tunneling (chapter 8), traffic control (chapter 9), and the security-hardening layer sitting across several of the earlier mechanisms at once (chapter 10). This final chapter's contribution has been narrower and, in a sense, more practical than any single mechanism: a method for moving from an unclassified symptom to whichever of the preceding ten chapters' specific tools the symptom actually calls for, rather than either guessing at a cause or working through every chapter's material regardless of relevance.

The habit worth carrying forward past this series' final page is the one section 2's layered elimination principle was built to instill: check the cheapest, most fundamental layer first, let each check's outcome — not intuition, not the most "interesting" hypothesis — determine the next step, and reach for the specific chapter whose mechanism actually explains the observed behavior, rather than treating "the network" as an undifferentiated black box whose failures require guesswork rather than derivation. That habit, more than any individual command or sysctl this series has covered, is the actual, lasting payoff of having built the full model these eleven chapters set out to construct.
